## Lemma SOL.1 — Legendre, conjugate, and gradient data

**Lemma SOL.1.** Let
\[
g(s)=
\begin{cases}
s\ln s-s,&s>0,\\
0,&s=0,\\
+\infty,&s<0,
\end{cases}
\qquad
f(x)=g(x_1)+g(x_2).
\]
Then:

1. \(f\) is Legendre;
2. \(\dom f=\mathbb R^2_+\), so \(\dom f\neq X\);
3. \(U=\operatorname{int}\dom f=\mathbb R^2_{++}\);
4. for \(x\in U\),
   \[
   \nabla f(x)=(\ln x_1,\ln x_2);
   \]
5. 
   \[
   f^*(u)=e^{u_1}+e^{u_2},\qquad
   \dom f^*=\mathbb R^2,\qquad U^*=\mathbb R^2;
   \]
6. \(\nabla f:U\to U^*\) is a bijection, with inverse
   \[
   (\nabla f)^{-1}(u)=(e^{u_1},e^{u_2}).
   \]

**Proof.**

For \(s>0\),
\[
g'(s)=\ln s,\qquad g''(s)=\frac1s>0.
\]
Thus \(g\) is strictly convex on \(]0,+\infty[\). Strict convexity also holds when one endpoint is \(0\): if \(b>0\) and \(0<\lambda<1\), then
\[
g(\lambda b)
=\lambda g(b)+\lambda b\ln\lambda
<\lambda g(b)+(1-\lambda)g(0),
\]
because \(\ln\lambda<0\). Also,
\[
\lim_{s\downarrow0}(s\ln s-s)=0=g(0),
\]
so \(g\) is lower semicontinuous on \(\mathbb R\). Therefore \(f\) is proper, lower semicontinuous, and convex, with
\[
\dom f=\mathbb R^2_+,\qquad U=\mathbb R^2_{++}.
\]
For example, \((-1,0)\notin\dom f\), so \(\dom f\neq X\).

On \(U\),
\[
\nabla f(x)=(\ln x_1,\ln x_2),
\qquad
\nabla^2f(x)=
\begin{pmatrix}
1/x_1&0\\
0&1/x_2
\end{pmatrix},
\]
and the Hessian is positive definite.

If \(x^k\in U\) converges to a boundary point of \(\dom f\), at least one coordinate tends to \(0\), and hence the corresponding component of \(\nabla f(x^k)\) tends to \(-\infty\). Thus
\[
\|\nabla f(x^k)\|\longrightarrow+\infty.
\]
This proves essential smoothness.

Moreover, no boundary point of \(\dom f\) has a finite subgradient. Indeed, suppose \(x_j=0\) and \(p\in\partial f(x)\). Replacing only the \(j\)-th coordinate by \(s>0\) in the subgradient inequality gives
\[
s\ln s-s\ge p_js.
\]
After division by \(s\),
\[
\ln s-1\ge p_j
\quad\text{for every }s>0.
\]
Taking \(s=e^{p_j}\) yields \(p_j-1\ge p_j\), a contradiction. Hence
\[
\dom\partial f=U.
\]
Since \(f\) is strictly convex on \(U\), it is essentially strictly convex. Therefore \(f\) is Legendre.

For \(u\in\mathbb R\), consider
\[
us-g(s)=s(u+1-\ln s),\qquad s>0.
\]
Its derivative is \(u-\ln s\), and its second derivative is \(-1/s<0\). Its unique maximizer is \(s=e^u\), where its value is \(e^u\). The boundary value at \(s=0\) is \(0<e^u\). Consequently,
\[
g^*(u)=e^u.
\]
Separability gives
\[
f^*(u)=e^{u_1}+e^{u_2}.
\]
Thus \(\dom f^*=\mathbb R^2\), so \(U^*=\mathbb R^2\). Finally,
\[
\nabla f(x)=(\ln x_1,\ln x_2)
\]
is bijective from \(\mathbb R^2_{++}\) to \(\mathbb R^2\), with coordinatewise exponential as inverse. ∎

**Additional standing-condition check.** If one reads the source with an additional \(1\)-coercivity or supercoercivity standing assumption, this example also satisfies it. Indeed, \(g(s)\ge-1\) for \(s\ge0\). If \(M=\max\{x_1,x_2\}\) and \(r=\|x\|_2\), then
\[
f(x)\ge M\ln M-M-1,\qquad r\le\sqrt2\,M.
\]
For \(M\ge e^2\), the right-hand side is positive, and
\[
\frac{f(x)}{\|x\|_2}
\ge
\frac{\ln M-1-1/M}{\sqrt2}\longrightarrow+\infty.
\]

## Lemma SOL.2 — Exact analytic curvature bound

**Lemma SOL.2.** For
\[
q(t)=e^t+(4t^2-2)e^{-t^2},\qquad 1\le t\le2,
\]
one has
\[
\inf_{t\in[1,2]}q(t)
=q(1)
=e+\frac2e
>\frac{41}{12}
>\frac{17}{5}>0.
\]

**Proof.**

Differentiate:
\[
q'(t)
=e^t+4t(3-2t^2)e^{-t^2}.
\]
Set
\[
\alpha=\sqrt{\frac32}.
\]

### First interval: \(1\le t\le\alpha\)

Here \(3-2t^2\ge0\). Therefore
\[
q'(t)\ge e^t>0.
\]

### Second interval: \(\alpha\le t\le\frac32\)

Set
\[
p(t)=4t(2t^2-3)=8t^3-12t.
\]
Then
\[
p'(t)=24t^2-12>0
\]
on this interval, and
\[
0\le p(t)\le p\left(\frac32\right)=9.
\]
Also \(t^2\ge3/2\), so
\[
e^{-t^2}\le e^{-3/2}.
\]
The positive-term exponential series gives
\[
e^{3/2}
>
1+\frac32+\frac{(3/2)^2}{2!}+\frac{(3/2)^3}{3!}
=\frac{67}{16}>4.
\]
Hence \(e^{-3/2}<1/4\). Also
\[
e>1+1+\frac12=\frac52.
\]
It follows that
\[
q'(t)=e^t-p(t)e^{-t^2}
>\frac52-\frac94
=\frac14>0.
\]

### Third interval: \(\frac32\le t\le2\)

The same polynomial \(p\) is increasing, so
\[
0\le p(t)\le p(2)=40.
\]
Moreover,
\[
e^t\ge e^{3/2},
\qquad
e^{-t^2}\le e^{-9/4}.
\]
Therefore
\[
q'(t)\ge e^{3/2}-40e^{-9/4}
=e^{-9/4}\bigl(e^{15/4}-40\bigr).
\]
It remains to prove \(e^{15/4}>40\) without numerical approximation. The positive-term exponential series gives
\[
e^3>
\sum_{k=0}^8\frac{3^k}{k!}
=\frac{806769}{40320}
=20+\frac{369}{40320}
>20,
\]
and
\[
e^{3/4}>
1+\frac34+\frac{(3/4)^2}{2}
=\frac{65}{32}>2.
\]
Consequently,
\[
e^{15/4}=e^3e^{3/4}>20\cdot2=40.
\]
Thus \(q'(t)>0\) also on \([3/2,2]\).

We have proved \(q'(t)>0\) throughout \([1,2]\). By the mean value theorem, \(q\) is strictly increasing, and hence
\[
\inf_{t\in[1,2]}q(t)=q(1)=e+\frac2e.
\]

Finally, the positive-term series gives
\[
e>
1+1+\frac12+\frac16
=\frac83.
\]
For \(s\ge8/3\), the function
\[
F(s)=s+\frac2s
\]
satisfies
\[
F'(s)=1-\frac2{s^2}
\ge1-\frac9{32}
=\frac{23}{32}>0.
\]
Therefore
\[
e+\frac2e
=F(e)
>F\left(\frac83\right)
=\frac83+\frac34
=\frac{41}{12}.
\]
Moreover,
\[
\frac{41}{12}-\frac{17}{5}
=\frac1{60}>0.
\]
This proves the stated bound. ∎

## Lemma SOL.3 — The set \(C\) and the closure condition

**Lemma SOL.3.** Let
\[
c(t)=(e^t,e^{-t^2}),\qquad
C=c([1,2]).
\]
Then \(C\) is nonempty, compact, closed in \(X\), and contained in \(U\). Moreover,
\[
C^*=\nabla f(C)=\{(t,-t^2):1\le t\le2\},
\]
and
\[
\operatorname{cl}C^*=C^*\subset U^*=\mathbb R^2.
\]

**Proof.**

The map \(c:[1,2]\to\mathbb R^2\) is continuous, and \([1,2]\) is compact. Thus \(C\) is compact and therefore closed in \(\mathbb R^2\). It is nonempty because \(c(1)\in C\).

For \(1\le t\le2\),
\[
e^t>0,\qquad e^{-t^2}>0,
\]
so \(c(t)\in\mathbb R^2_{++}=U\).

Using Lemma SOL.1,
\[
\nabla f(c(t))
=\bigl(\ln(e^t),\ln(e^{-t^2})\bigr)
=(t,-t^2).
\]
Hence
\[
C^*=\{(t,-t^2):t\in[1,2]\}.
\]
This is another continuous image of the compact interval \([1,2]\), so it is compact and closed. Since \(U^*=\mathbb R^2\),
\[
\operatorname{cl}C^*=C^*\subset U^*.
\]
Finally, \(c\) is injective because its first coordinate \(e^t\) is strictly increasing. ∎

## Lemma SOL.4 — Exact right-divergence reduction

**Lemma SOL.4.** For every \(x=(x_1,x_2)\in U\) and every \(t\in[1,2]\),
\[
D_f(x,c(t))=K(x)+h_x(t),
\]
where
\[
K(x)=x_1\ln x_1-x_1+x_2\ln x_2-x_2=f(x)
\]
and
\[
h_x(t)=e^t+e^{-t^2}-x_1t+x_2t^2.
\]

**Proof.**

For \(x,y\in U\), coordinatewise expansion of the Bregman divergence gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
D_f(x,y)
&=\sum_{j=1}^2
\left[
x_j\ln x_j-x_j
-(y_j\ln y_j-y_j)
-\ln(y_j)(x_j-y_j)
\right]\\
&=\sum_{j=1}^2
\left[
x_j\ln\left(\frac{x_j}{y_j}\right)-x_j+y_j
\right].
\end{aligned}
\]
For \(y=c(t)=(e^t,e^{-t^2})\), this becomes
\[
\begin{aligned}
D_f(x,c(t))
&=x_1\ln\left(\frac{x_1}{e^t}\right)-x_1+e^t
+x_2\ln\left(\frac{x_2}{e^{-t^2}}\right)-x_2+e^{-t^2}\\
&=x_1\ln x_1-x_1t-x_1+e^t\\
&\quad+x_2\ln x_2+x_2t^2-x_2+e^{-t^2}\\
&=\bigl(x_1\ln x_1-x_1+x_2\ln x_2-x_2\bigr)\\
&\quad+\bigl(e^t+e^{-t^2}-x_1t+x_2t^2\bigr).
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus the \(t\)-independent constant is exactly \(K(x)=f(x)\). ∎

## Lemma SOL.5 — Global right \(D_f\)-Chebyshevness, including endpoints

**Lemma SOL.5.** For every \(x\in U=\mathbb R^2_{++}\), the function \(h_x\) has a unique minimizer on \([1,2]\). Consequently,
\[
P^{\to}_C(x)=\arg\min_{y\in C}D_f(x,y)
\]
is a singleton for every \(x\in U\).

**Proof.**

Differentiation gives
\[
h_x'(t)=e^t-2te^{-t^2}-x_1+2x_2t
\]
and
\[
h_x''(t)
=e^t+(4t^2-2)e^{-t^2}+2x_2
=q(t)+2x_2.
\]
By Lemma SOL.2,
\[
q(t)>\frac{17}{5}
\qquad(1\le t\le2).
\]
Since \(x_2>0\),
\[
h_x''(t)>\frac{17}{5}>0
\qquad(1\le t\le2).
\]
Thus \(h_x\) is strictly convex on \([1,2]\).

Because \(h_x\) is continuous and \([1,2]\) is compact, it attains a minimum. If two distinct points \(s,t\in[1,2]\) were both minimizers, strict convexity would give
\[
h_x\!\left(\frac{s+t}{2}\right)
<
\frac{h_x(s)+h_x(t)}2
=\min_{[1,2]}h_x,
\]
which is impossible. Hence the minimizer is unique.

The minimizer need not be interior. Since \(h_x'\) is strictly increasing, the endpoint cases are:

1. If \(h_x'(1)\ge0\), then \(h_x'(t)>0\) for every \(t>1\), and the unique minimizer is \(t=1\).

2. If \(h_x'(2)\le0\), then \(h_x'(t)<0\) for every \(t<2\), and the unique minimizer is \(t=2\).

3. Otherwise,
   \[
   h_x'(1)<0<h_x'(2).
   \]
   The intermediate value theorem gives a zero \(t_x\in(1,2)\), and strict monotonicity of \(h_x'\) makes it unique. This \(t_x\) is the unique minimizer.

These cases are exhaustive because \(h_x'(1)<h_x'(2)\).

By Lemma SOL.4, minimizing \(D_f(x,c(t))\) is exactly the same as minimizing \(h_x(t)\), since \(K(x)\) is independent of \(t\). By injectivity of \(c\), the unique minimizing parameter determines a unique point of \(C\). Therefore
\[
P^{\to}_C(x)=\{c(t_x)\}
\]
for every \(x\in U\). Thus \(C\) is globally right \(D_f\)-Chebyshev in the stipulated sense. ∎

## Lemma SOL.6 — Explicit nonconvexity witness for \(C^*\)

**Lemma SOL.6.** The set
\[
C^*=\{(t,-t^2):1\le t\le2\}
\]
is not convex.

**Proof.**

The two points
\[
u=(1,-1),\qquad v=(2,-4)
\]
belong to \(C^*\). Their midpoint is
\[
m=\frac{u+v}{2}
=\left(\frac32,-\frac52\right).
\]
If \(m\in C^*\), its first coordinate would force \(t=3/2\). The corresponding second coordinate on \(C^*\) would then be
\[
-\left(\frac32\right)^2=-\frac94,
\]
whereas
\[
-\frac52=-\frac{10}{4}\neq-\frac94.
\]
Hence \(m\notin C^*\), proving nonconvexity. ∎

## Lemma SOL.7 — \(C\) itself is also nonconvex

**Lemma SOL.7.** The original set
\[
C=\{(e^t,e^{-t^2}):1\le t\le2\}
\]
is not convex.

**Proof.**

Writing \(r=e^t\), \(C\) is the graph over \(r\in[e,e^2]\) of
\[
\psi(r)=e^{-(\ln r)^2}.
\]
Let \(L=\ln r\). Direct differentiation gives
\[
\psi''(r)
=
\frac{e^{-L^2}}{r^2}\bigl(4L^2+2L-2\bigr).
\]
For \(L\in[1,2]\),
\[
4L^2+2L-2
=4+(L-1)(4L+6)\ge4>0.
\]
Thus \(\psi''(r)>0\) on \(]e,e^2[\), so \(\psi\) is strictly convex on \([e,e^2]\).

Consider
\[
a=c(1)=(e,e^{-1}),\qquad
b=c(2)=(e^2,e^{-4}),
\]
and their midpoint
\[
M=\left(\frac{e+e^2}{2},\frac{e^{-1}+e^{-4}}{2}\right).
\]
Set \(r_0=(e+e^2)/2\). Strict convexity gives
\[
\psi(r_0)
<
\frac{\psi(e)+\psi(e^2)}2
=
\frac{e^{-1}+e^{-4}}2.
\]
The unique point of \(C\) with first coordinate \(r_0\) has second coordinate \(\psi(r_0)\), not the second coordinate of \(M\). Hence \(M\notin C\), so \(C\) is nonconvex. ∎

This does not violate Fact 3.2: the quoted fact does not assume that \(C\) is convex, and its conclusion concerns \(C^*\), not \(C\).

## Theorem SOL.8 — Exact consequence for Fact 3.2

**Theorem SOL.8.** In \(X=\mathbb R^2\), let \(f\) be negative entropy and let
\[
C=\{(e^t,e^{-t^2}):1\le t\le2\}.
\]
Then:

1. \(f\) is Legendre;
2. \(\dom f=\mathbb R^2_+\neq X\), so hypothesis (a) of Fact 3.2 fails;
3. \(C\) is nonempty, closed, and contained in \(U\);
4. \(\operatorname{cl}C^*\subset U^*\), so hypothesis (b) holds;
5. \(C\) is right \(D_f\)-Chebyshev for every \(x\in U\), so hypothesis (c) holds;
6. \(C^*\) is nonconvex.

Consequently, the implication in Fact 3.2 becomes false if hypothesis (a), \(\dom f=X\), is simply deleted while all the other quoted hypotheses are retained.

**Proof.**

Items 1–5 follow from Lemmas SOL.1–SOL.5, and item 6 follows from Lemma SOL.6. Therefore the weakened implication
\[
\text{(b) and (c)}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
C^*\text{ is convex}
\]
is false for general Legendre functions whose domains need not equal \(X\). ∎
